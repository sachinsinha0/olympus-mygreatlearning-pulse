# The AI Pulse landing page, told as the reader's story

_Copy deck. 2 September 2026._

> **Outcome, recorded 2 September.** Three of the six proposed changes were reversed
> after review, and the reasoning is worth keeping. The recognition section and the
> vetting concession were cut as too much to read, and the ratings row went back to
> second on the page to build trust early. The audience is the reason: this lead
> arrives from a sales call already knowing what Pulse is, so a beat that establishes
> the problem is work they do not need, and a brand they already half trust is worth
> confirming immediately. The arc below still describes the reasoning; sections 2, 4
> and the ratings move are not what shipped. What did ship: the trial heading reframed
> to the reader's outcome, and the FAQ resequenced with two entries added.

## Who is reading

A working professional. A Great Learning salesperson called them about the PG Program
this week, mentioned a free 14 day trial of AI Pulse, and sent a link. So they arrive
warm but unsure, and they are not shopping for a course. They have a job.

What they actually feel, which the page has never named: **everyone around them seems to
be using AI at work and they are not sure they are keeping up.** That is the feeling the
page has to meet in its first ten seconds. Not "AI is transforming industries", which
tells them nothing they do not know.

## What is wrong with the page as a story today

The order is hero, ratings, what it is, the cadence, what is inside, modules, trial, PG,
FAQ. Read as a narrative it has five problems.

1. **The reader's problem is never stated.** The page opens on our positioning, "AI moves
   fast, Pulse keeps you in sync", and goes straight to explaining the product. There is
   no moment where the reader thinks *that is me*.
2. **Three explaining sections in a row.** What it is, the cadence, what is inside. That
   is the page telling rather than showing, and over-explaining is the single strongest
   marker of machine-written copy.
3. **Ratings sit second**, asking for trust before the reader has any interest to protect.
4. **Objections wait until the end.** "I have no time" arrives in the reader's head on
   about line three, not after nine sections.
5. **Nothing is conceded.** Every section is a claim. Human writing admits limits, and a
   page with no limits reads like a brochure.

## The arc

Seven beats. The reader moves from *that is me* to *this is small enough to try tonight*.

### 1. The tension. Hero, unchanged.

"AI moves fast. Pulse keeps you in sync." Product's own words, already settled. It states
the tension in six words, which is the right job for a hero.

### 2. Recognition. New section.

The reader's week, in their words, before we say anything about ourselves.

> **There is a new AI tool every week. You have a job.**
>
> You cannot test all of them. Most of what you read tells you a tool exists, not whether
> it is worth your afternoon.
>
> YouTube has everything, in no order. A forty hour course assumes you have forty hours.
> Newsletters tell you what shipped, not how to use it.

Why this copy: it names three real alternatives instead of gesturing at "other resources",
which is what human writing does and machine writing avoids. "You have a job" is four
words and does the work of a paragraph. No sentence explains the previous sentence.

### 3. The turn. What Pulse is.

The existing beat, product's words, now landing as an answer to a question the reader has
already been asked.

> **Your AI learning companion**
> Learn new AI tools and how you can use them at work.

### 4. The promise, with a concession. New copy inside the cadence section.

> **Learn one cutting-edge AI tool or innovation, every two weeks**
> Up to 60 minutes each. Short enough to fit your schedule, deep enough to apply at work
> immediately.
>
> Someone here uses the tool on real work before it becomes a module. If it does not hold
> up, we drop it and you never see it.

Why add this: the human vetting gate is the strongest thing in
`docs/great-learning-pulse-brief.md` and it appears nowhere on the page. It is also the
only line that concedes something, we throw topics away, which is what makes the rest
believable.

### 5. Proof. Modules, labs, topics.

Real module titles, real lab logos, real topics. Nothing to write here. The page already
shows rather than claims, which is its best existing section.

### 6. The small ask. Trial section, reframed.

Today it is headed "How does the free trial work?", which describes our mechanism. The
reader's question is what they get and when.

> **You can finish your first module tonight**
>
> Log in with your email. No card and no forms.
> Open any of the eleven modules.
> Finish it in under an hour.
>
> After 14 days you can subscribe to keep going. Everything you finished stays yours.

### 7. Objections, in the reader's order. FAQ resequenced.

Today: what it is, who for, credit card, after 14 days, module time, PG. That is our
order. The reader worries in this order:

1. How much time does a module take? (time is the first objection, always)
2. Do I need to know how to code?
3. Do I need a credit card to start?
4. What happens after 14 days?
5. How is this different from free videos and newsletters?
6. How does this relate to the PG Program?

Two of these are missing today. "Do I need to code" is buried inside the who-is-it-for
answer, and the YouTube question is not asked at all even though it is the objection every
reader has.

### 8. The bigger door. PG Program, last.

It stays where it is, after the small commitment has been resolved. Asking for a seven
month programme before the reader has agreed to a free fortnight is the wrong order, and
that order is already correct on the page.

## What is deliberately not changed

- **The hero.** Settled, and the words are the product's.
- **The three pitch beats' own titles and bodies.** Product verbatim.
- **Module content.** Real data.

## One line I would change but cannot without a decision

The hero subtitle is product verbatim:

> "A biweekly learning module on the new AI tools, innovations, and workflows reshaping
> work, distilled into 60 minutes of learning that you can actually apply."

It is the least spoken sentence on the page. Four abstract nouns chained together, and
"distilled" is a word nobody says out loud. In the reader's register it would be:

> "One new AI tool every two weeks. Under an hour each. Use it at work the same day."

Same facts, three short sentences, no bookish words. But it is the product's line and the
banner is settled, so this needs a call rather than an edit.
